While reading Collingwood's posthumous '*The Idea of History*', I came across a view which he terms the 'escapist' view of history, or historical 'escapism' during his critique of Decartes. Collingwood defines Historical Escapism as it appears in Decartes as:

> Historical escapism: the historian is a traveller who by living away from home becomes  stranger to his own age. [^2]

This he defines among other positions Decartes takes, historical pyrrhonism, anti-utilitarian idea of history, and history as fantasy building. Only escapism interests me here, and is best summed up by a quote from Decartes' *Discourse on Method*:

> But those who travel too long end by being strangers in their own homes, and those who study too curiously the actions of antiquity are ignorant of what is done among our selves today [^1]

Collingwood's response to this position is that a historian must (and can only) study the past relative to his position in his age, we are not 'travelling', or leaping clean of our age, as much as we would like to:

> One answer to the 'escapist' view of history would be to show that the historian can genuinely see into the past only so far as he stands firmly rooted in the present; that is, his business is not to leap clean out of his own period of history but to be in every respect a man of his age and to see the past as it appears from the standpoint of that age [^2]

and in another passage:

> The historian (and for that matter the philosopher) is not God, looking at the world from above and outside. He is a man, and a man of his own time and place. He looks at the pas from the point of view of the present: he looks at other countries and civilisations from the point of view of his own. [^3]

He blames this position on the fact that the idea of knowledge in Decartes' time had not advanced to the point of conceiving knowledge '*as directed to an object relative to the knower's own point of view*'[^2]. This he says, would come with Kant.

[^2]: Collingwood, The Idea of History, ff. 59
