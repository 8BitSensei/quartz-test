I was recently reading Dillon's '*The Middle Platonists*'[^1] when he quoted a passage of Plutarch's '*On the Obsolescence of Oracles*' to express Xenocrates' triadic division of the universe, which is here reflected in his understanding of Daemons:

> As an illustration of this subject, Xenocrates, the companion of Plato, employed the order of triangles; the equilateral he compared to the nature of the Gods, the scalene to that of man, and isosceles to that of daemons; for the first is equal in all its lines, the second unequal in all, and the third is partly equal and unequal, like the nature of the daemons, which possesses human emotion and divine power. (Fr. 23)

This immediately reminded me of a passage I had recently read in Reeve's collection of texts on Aristotle's Theology [^2],  in the passage Aristotle is puzzling over why it is *not* the case, that those bodies closest to the primary mover have the simplest movements, and those furthest away the most. The example he gives is that the stars have more movements than the sun and moon. He goes on to say that we should conceive of the stars as taking part in *action and life*, for it all to make sense, and goes on with:

> For it seems that the good belongs *without action* to what is in the best state, to what is closest by means of *one small action*, and to what is further away by means of *several actions*. It is just as in the case of the body: one is in a good state without exercising, another by walking around a little, a third needs running, wrestling and hard training, while to another this good would not yet belong no matter how much exertion he undergoes, but rather a distinct one. (*De Caelo* 2.12.291b24-293a14)

To begin with, the good belongs to what is in its best state *without action*. This is the position of Xenocrates' godly equilateral triangle, it is in its best state, it cannot nor will not make any movements, any such change would be a degradation, this is not the case for the isosceles or scalene. Different bodies require different numbers of movements to reach a good state, one just needs take a regular walk, another hard training, much like Xenocrates' daemonic and human triangles.

But this is where the models diverge, for Aristotle goes on to say that another body would not be improved by exercise at all, this good will not belong to them, for them a distinct good, and so distinct actions are required. He points out that it seems that the actions towards goodness that humans take are most numerous, more so than animals, plants, and the stars, and that is why:

> \[...\] one should think the action of the stars to be like that of animals and plants. (*ibid*)

So, are plants an animals *more* divine than us? Are they closer to the heavenly bodies? Not so says Aristotle, it appears this way because we are not all attempting  to *fully attain* the good. 

> One thing, then, has and participates in the best, one reaches close to it by means of few steps, another by means of many, and another does not even try, but it is sufficient for it to come close to the ultimate end. (*ibid*)

And that we all have different starting places.

> \[...\] for one should understand that in the case of each one's life and starting point the primary one has a great superiority over the others \[...\] (*ibid*)

So, it should appear to us that plants have simple movements, less even than the stars, because not everything is trying to achieve the same ultimate good, nor are they starting from same point. So perhaps plants move little, because they are able to easily achieve the greatest good they can, which is a great distant from the ultimate good, and humans move a great amount, because we are *trying* to achieve a high good, despite a very lowly starting point. Perhaps a similar analogy is true for the stars and the sun and moon. We cannot take the simplicity or complexity of bodily movement, as a simple measure of closeness to the divine, it is instead a factor of where you start, and how high you set your goals. While Xenocrates' model is not wrong for Aristotle, it is gross oversimplification, which perhaps true for Humans, Daemons, and Gods, that are attempting to attain the same ultimate good, it does not hold for anything beyond that assumption.
